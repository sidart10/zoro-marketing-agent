# Content-type contracts

Choose one contract based on the requested deliverable. Length follows the user's constraint; when
none is given, prefer the shortest version that fully develops the idea.

## X short post

Use for one self-contained idea.

1. Opening: a concrete claim, observation, tension, or useful question.
2. Development: the mechanism, evidence, contrast, or example that earns the claim.
3. Landing: the implication, decision, or memorable synthesis.

Keep the opening understandable without a curiosity gap. Use line breaks to separate turns in the
idea, not after every sentence. Hashtags, engagement bait, and “agree?” are opt-in—not defaults.

## X thread

Use when the argument needs ordered development.

1. Post 1 stands alone: thesis + specific promise or stakes.
2. Post 2 frames the problem, terms, or decision criteria.
3. Middle posts each do one job: explain, prove, demonstrate, compare, or qualify.
4. Put strong evidence and the most useful example before the final third.
5. Close by compressing the argument into an implication or next action.

Numbering is optional. Do not repeat the opening in every post, create artificial cliffhangers, or
turn one paragraph into a thread by inserting line breaks.

## LinkedIn post

Use for professional insight that benefits from context and application.

1. Opening: name the costly misconception, observed pattern, or decision.
2. Reasoning: explain why it happens in plain language.
3. Practical value: offer a framework, example, checklist, or operating rule.
4. Boundary: state when the advice does not apply.
5. Close: a grounded implication; ask a question only when a real discussion would improve the idea.

Use whitespace for readability without making every sentence its own paragraph. Default to no emojis
or hashtags unless the user or brand voice asks for them.

## Newsletter or article

Use for a durable argument or deep explanation.

1. Title: precise promise or thesis.
2. Optional deck: who it is for and why the question matters.
3. Lead: a concrete situation, consequential change, or unresolved question.
4. Thesis: the answer the piece will support.
5. Body sections: one claim each, backed by evidence and connected by explicit reasoning.
6. Objection or limitation: strengthen trust by marking the boundary.
7. Conclusion: synthesize; do not merely recap section headings.

Use descriptive subheads. Preserve source links or endnotes for factual claims. Long-form depth comes
from developed reasoning, not longer introductions.

## Informative YouTube script

Use for explainers, tutorials, comparisons, and reviews.

1. **Opening:** problem, payoff, and reason to keep watching.
2. **Frame:** define the question, audience, and any essential terms.
3. **Roadmap:** preview the journey only when it reduces confusion.
4. **Body:** alternate explanation with proof, demonstration, or concrete examples.
5. **Friction:** address the obvious objection, limitation, or failure mode.
6. **Payoff:** resolve the opening and state the decision or lesson.
7. **CTA:** include only when requested or naturally connected to the viewer's next step.

For a timed script, estimate 130–155 spoken words per minute and adjust for pauses, demonstrations,
and dense terminology. Add A-roll, B-roll, on-screen text, or demo cues only when requested or when
visuals materially clarify the idea. Every visual cue should show something the narration cannot.

## Short-form video script

Use for one useful idea, usually delivered in under 90 seconds.

1. Hook: name the result, mistake, or surprising mechanism immediately.
2. Context: one or two lines—only what the payoff needs.
3. Value: demonstrate the steps, contrast, or example.
4. Payoff: complete the promise from the hook.
5. CTA: optional and specific.

Write for speech. Read it aloud, remove subordinate clauses that are hard to follow, and put key
visuals beside the exact line they support.
